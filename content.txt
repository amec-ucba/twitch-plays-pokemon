Page title: Twitch Plays Pokémon: A Million Players, One Game

Page description: The story of Twitch Plays Pokémon, the 2014 stream where more than a million people played one game of Pokémon Red together through chat.

Twitch Plays Pokémon

A million players. One Game Boy game. Sixteen days of chaos.

A Social Experiment

In February 2014, an anonymous programmer from Australia started a stream on Twitch with a strange idea: what if the chat played the game? They called it a social experiment, and they wrote a small program that read every message in the stream's chat and turned it into a button press. Then they loaded up Pokémon Red, a role-playing game (RPG) first released in Japan in 1996, and went live.

The creator picked Pokémon for a reason. The battles are turn-based, so nothing bad happens if a player is slow to react. And the game is forgiving. Even a group of players pressing buttons almost at random will eventually make some progress.

For the first day, almost nobody noticed. Then word spread. Within a few days, tens of thousands of people were watching at the same time, and news sites around the world were writing about it. Fans started calling it TPP for short.

How It Worked

Anyone watching could join in by typing one of eight commands into the chat: up, down, left, right, A, B, start, or select. Every message was a button press. Picture one Game Boy being passed around to everyone on the internet at the same time, and you get the idea.

With thousands of people typing at once, the chat moved faster than anyone could read. To make things harder, there was a delay between typing a command and seeing it happen on the stream. Even players who knew exactly where to go were always a few steps behind everyone else.

Not everyone was trying to help. Trolls figured out that spamming start would open the menu over and over and stop the game in its tracks. The streamer eventually had to slow down how often the start button could be pressed, and someone even built a program to spot the worst trolls automatically.

Anarchy vs. Democracy

On February 18, after the crowd got badly stuck on a puzzle, the streamer changed the rules. Players could now vote on how the game was played by typing anarchy or democracy in the chat. A meter on the screen showed which side was winning.

Anarchy Every command happens in the order it arrives. It is fast, loud, and completely unpredictable. Somehow, it usually works.

Democracy The chat votes every few seconds, and only the most popular command happens. It is organized and much better at solving puzzles, but it is painfully slow.

Many fans hated Democracy. To them, the chaos was the whole point, and the fight between the two modes became one of the biggest stories of the stream.

The Lore

When something makes no sense, people make up a story to explain it. The TPP crowd did exactly that. Over sixteen days, they turned the game's Pokémon and items into a full mythology of heroes, villains, and prophets.

Lord Helix

Early in the game, the crowd picked up the Helix Fossil, an item that does nothing until much later. Because the chat kept opening the menu by accident, the player kept trying to use the fossil over and over. Fans decided it must be giving them advice. When the fossil was finally revived as a Pokémon called Omanyte, the chat crowned it Lord Helix, and fans began to treat it like a god. Whenever something went right, the chat filled up with Praise Helix. The fossil the crowd did not pick, the Dome Fossil, became its evil opposite.

Bird Jesus

Bird Jesus was a Pidgeot, and for most of the game it was the strongest Pokémon on the team by far. When the rest of the team fell apart, Bird Jesus kept winning battles almost by itself. Fans saw it as the chosen protector of Lord Helix.

The False Prophet

The crowd needed a Water-type Pokémon to cross the sea, and the plan was to evolve an Eevee into Vaporeon using a Water Stone. Instead, the chat used the wrong stone, and Eevee became Flareon, a Fire-type that was no help at all. Flareon was named the False Prophet, and fans blamed it for nearly everything that went wrong afterward.

To sum it up: the plan was to evolve Eevee into Vaporeon Flareon.

Bloody Sunday

The darkest day of the run happened on Sunday, February 23. The crowd was trying to move Pokémon around in the game's storage system when the chaos took over. By the time it was done, the chat had accidentally released about a dozen Pokémon, including the very first Pokémon the team ever had. They were gone forever. Players still talk about it more than ten years later.

The Final Battle

After Bloody Sunday, many people expected the run to fall apart. It did not. The crowd regrouped, rebuilt the team, and kept pushing forward. On March 1, 2014, after more than sixteen straight days of play, the chat defeated the Elite Four and the final rival. Tens of thousands of people were watching live when the credits rolled.

By the Numbers

1,165,140 people took part 121,000 people watched at the same time at its peak 55 million total views 16 days to beat the game

Timeline

February 12, 2014: The stream goes live with Pokémon Red. February 18, 2014: Democracy mode is added. February 23, 2014: Bloody Sunday. March 1, 2014: The crowd beats the game. March 2, 2014: A new run begins with Pokémon Crystal. December 2014: TPP wins Best Fan Creation at The Game Awards.

What It Left Behind

TPP did not stop after Pokémon Red. The channel moved on to Pokémon Crystal the very next day, and then to game after game for years. It also inspired a wave of copycat streams where chat played other games, from Super Mario Bros. to Dark Souls. Someone even set up a stream where a fish swimming around its tank chose the button presses.

Twitch learned something too. Viewers did not just want to watch. They wanted to play, and interactive streams became a bigger part of the platform from then on.

The stream also earned a Guinness World Records title for:

Most participants on a single-player online videogame
